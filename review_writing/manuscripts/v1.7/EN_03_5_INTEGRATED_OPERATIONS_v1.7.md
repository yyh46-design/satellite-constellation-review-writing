## 3.5 Coupling Across Management Problems and Integrated Operations

Service scheduling, capacity configuration, and execution management interact through mission dependencies, availability, and changing operational states. Surveys of automation tools [6](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2020.08.018) and trusted autonomous operations [105](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paerosci.2023.100960) discuss the organizational and implementation aspects of these connections. Coupling can be implemented as joint decisions inside one model, a sequential input passed between models, or plan revisions triggered by observed execution states. Software architectures assign responsibility for representing and acting on this information. These mechanisms differ in variables shared, decisions made together, and feedback timing.

### Task allocation and data-delivery paths

Contact-graph scheduling by Lowe et al. [106](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adhoc.2023.103289) searches for mission request transfer, acquisition, and eventual data delivery after each request arrives. The acquisition satellite is selected according to the earliest expected delivery, not only imaging availability. A resource-aware variant subtracts previously committed contact capacity before selecting subsequent paths. The approach solves requests sequentially rather than optimizing all requests simultaneously and assumes unconstrained storage and energy. In the examined 16-satellite simulation, resource-aware and source-routing options increase nominal deliveries but can also raise mean delay among *successfully delivered* requests; local routing is more resilient under selected contact failures. Preplanned paths avoid some congestion but may allow data to expire if a contact fails. Because additional deliveries also change the composition of successful samples, delivery count and conditional mean delay must be interpreted jointly.

The acquisition-to-processing framework [53](https://doi.org/10.1002/sat.70082) first constructs acquisition schedules and passes acquired workload and transmission windows to a downstream allocation problem for processing loads and operating frequencies across satellite and ground processors, accounting for transmission energy and capacity. Offloading and cooperative-inference approaches [54](https://doi.org/10.1109/JIOT.2024.3482290), [55](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.comcom.2024.05.008), [56](https://doi.org/10.1109/TMC.2025.3570799) further optimize execution locations and inference resources. These approaches do not have the same coupling variables as per-request contact-graph acquisition/delivery routing. A sequential upstream input and a joint optimization of downstream resources can coexist; how much of the end-to-end chain is actually jointly decided depends on the formulation. The detailed description of [53] continues to rely on the specified public author version pending a journal-version comparison.

### Asset states and reconfiguration of service resources

Servicing, logistics, and propulsion models [27](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A34978), [28](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A35094), [23](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A36684) represent capacity through positions, available times, and remaining resources. Joint communication platform and constellation design [17](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.comnet.2024.110942) establishes traffic-related network capacity. Recovery and configuration results can enter operational resource allocation as the usable capability available in a region and time interval. However, linking the outputs of distinct models conceptually does not establish that they have been solved or validated as one optimization problem.

Singla et al. [9](https://doi.org/10.3390/technologies13080312) incorporate spacecraft resources and network service requirements into a unified mission model, construct initial schedules heuristically, improve them by local search, and replan after telemetry or task-failure events. Their SatOps–NetOps framework distinguishes **three interaction modes**: integrated operation with a unified plan, coordinated operation in which shared arrangements are executed through different channels, and adaptive operation in which managers exchange needs and plans but select actions separately. The article discusses **four concrete architectural arrangements** across those interaction modes. The main experimental comparison for the integrated prototype involves a 16-satellite simulation against semi-manual best-effort scheduling without the same service-objective optimization. It demonstrates the tested implementation's service performance, not a controlled ranking of all three interaction modes under one common scenario.

Hierarchical resource control [81](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25030902) partitions responsibilities for service regions, spectrum, and local beams, while Starlink's disclosed collision-avoidance process [89](https://space-safety.starlink.com/docs/space-safety-articles/starlink_cola_conops/) links risk updates to maneuver changes. These studies illustrate resource-responsibility allocation and feedback from physical actions, respectively. Coverage, capacity, and intervals of availability are possible interfaces between operations and network-resource reconfiguration; effects of maneuvers, exits, and derating on those variables remain questions requiring models containing the same service events.

### State feedback, plan updates, and coordinated execution

Feedback modifies future plans by changing candidate activities or scheduling preferences. A reasoning-based agile-observation method [107](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs15061577) considers a single satellite: it computes predicted observation, transmission, and electrical states under subsystem constraints, uses storage/energy rules to determine target priorities, and inserts feasible activities subject to attitude, downlink, and power checks. The corresponding methodology is supported by relevant sections and algorithm steps in the formal open HTML article. Its findings are based on a single-satellite model and do not establish learned prediction, flight telemetry feedback, or multiagent deployment. The Remote Agent architecture [108](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0004-3702%2898%2900068-X) links planning, execution, mode identification, and reconfiguration; EO-1 [109](https://doi.org/10.1109/SMC-IT.2006.48), IPEX [110](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.I010386), and the JPL IPEX record [111](https://ai.jpl.nasa.gov/public/projects/ipex/) provide single-spacecraft experience connecting onboard data processing, resources, and continuous planning. The first study maps modeled states to strategies, while the flight missions show limited operating functions in actual systems.

Software architectures further organize monitoring and decisions. A reference architecture for autonomy and adaptivity [112](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jss.2026.112802) applies the monitor–analyze–plan–execute/knowledge (MAPE-K) concepts, distinguishes application-level and operational responsibilities, and reports expert evaluation and architecture instantiation on NIMBUS. The publisher's public description and authors' summaries support these architectural facts, but the final full article was not accessible for detailed verification in this review round. They do not establish constellation-scale scheduling efficiency or autonomous flight deployment. A Schedule Manager [103](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.I011208) handles task admission and contingency schedules, while NASA's core Flight System demonstration package [113](https://github.com/nasa/cFS) supplies a software integration example. Passing state and plan information across modules provides an implementation substrate; actual resource-conflict resolution depends on each mission's model and execution rules.

Flight experiments distinguish internal constellation coordination from interaction between operators. NASA Starling [114](https://www.nasa.gov/smallspacecraft/what-is-starling/) uses four CubeSats to share observation states and coordinate subsequent GPS observations. In Starling 1.5 [115](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/ames/nasa-starling-and-spacex-starlink-improve-space-traffic-coordination/), [114](https://www.nasa.gov/smallspacecraft/what-is-starling/), operators exchange trajectories and risk information, determine maneuver responsibility, and execute an actual maneuver during a **simulated conjunction scenario**. The former concerns internal joint observation choice and the latter action responsibility across operators. Neither establishes the performance of an entire mega-constellation integrated management system.

Integrated-operation studies have implemented joint selection of tasks and paths, sequential transfer of plans between decision models, and revisions driven by monitored states. Joint selection includes downstream capacity in current feasibility, sequential planning fixes an upstream input to a later optimization, and feedback changes subsequent decisions after new information arrives. Their computational, informational, and revisability costs differ. Basilisk [116](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.I010762) and formal-methods discussions [117](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-06773-0_1) provide foundations for dynamic simulation, software, and specification checking. These distinctions motivate evaluation of additional coupling under common requests, traffic, and disturbances, rather than asserting that the surveyed components already form one verified system.
