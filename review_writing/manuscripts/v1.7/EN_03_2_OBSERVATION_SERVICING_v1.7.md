## 3.2 Earth-Observation and On-Orbit-Servicing Mission Scheduling

Earth-observation and servicing problems both involve selecting, assigning, and ordering activities, but candidate activities are generated differently. Observation opportunities depend on target visibility, attitude, and payload constraints; servicing visits also depend on transfers, propellant, and replenishment. As dynamic requests and multiple resources enter these models, scheduling extends from a fixed activity set to planning with updated opportunities and states.

### Observation request selection, assignment, and timing

Agile observation selection must account for target windows and attitude maneuvers. Lemaître et al. [30](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1270-9638%2802%2901173-2) compare greedy, dynamic programming, constraint programming, and local search, providing an early basis for integrated request selection and timing. A Markov decision-process formulation [31](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSMC.2020.3020732) learns an order of actions before a constructive heuristic assigns specific times. A decomposition-and-learning multiobjective method [32](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.swevo.2024.101792) combines task allocation, ordering, and global repair. Despite their different search organizations, these methods must convert selected requests into sequences satisfying attitude and time-window constraints.

When several satellites share conflicting requests, a mixed-integer model can express task selection, assignments, and time conflicts, but the search space grows with candidate assignments and activities. Antunes et al. [33](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cor.2025.107259) model public tasks and tasks exclusive to specified satellites under battery charging/discharging constraints. They compare two Dantzig–Wolfe decompositions, use dynamic programming in the pricing subproblems, and incorporate dual stabilization, strong branching, and integer programming heuristics. The resulting branch-and-price method exploits task and resource structure and retains mathematical optimization bounds for its discrete formulation. Its effectiveness depends on the structure and cost of pricing, rather than on satellite count alone.

Coordination among satellites also depends on the information each member receives. Parjan and Chien [34](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.I011215) compare centralized, broadcast-based decentralized, and highly decentralized allocations in a one-week scenario with 100 satellite agents and 634 ground targets. Broadcast alternatives exchange information about conflicts or the current request-satisfaction state. In their model with simplified capacities and fixed observation-conflict intervals, incremental planning reduces computation and increases repeated observations. The result must be understood together with request satisfaction and redundancy, rather than interpreted as proof that incremental planning necessarily reduces overall schedule quality. All agents are executed in a single-thread simulation, not measured as a real parallel onboard implementation.

Zilberstein et al. [35](https://doi.org/10.1613/jair.1.16997) exploit the geometry of observation opportunities and conflicts by modeling the problem as distributed constraint optimization (DCOP), decomposing it into geometric neighborhoods (GND), and coordinating these through neighborhood stochastic search (NSS). Agents share public requests and constellation geometry but do not have other satellites' complete operating capabilities. Experiments with 200 and 108 simulated satellites over 24 h include storage and downlink restrictions and report request satisfaction, computation, and message overhead. Parjan and Chien emphasize alternatives in shared information, while GND selects local problems according to the decisions that interact. In small request sets, GND may improve quality and overhead relative to a broadcast baseline; in larger sets a satisfaction–cost trade-off can emerge. Hence the structure and density of competition, not just the number of satellites, govern distributed performance.

The ownership of task requests and the conflict protocol provide further design dimensions. Krigman et al. [36](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10951-024-00816-x) model independently owned observation requests as a DCOP and employ distributed stochastic and max-gain messaging algorithms, reducing the need to centralize privately held constraints. Consensus-based bundle auctions [37](https://doi.org/10.1109/TRO.2009.2022423) combine local bidding and consensus to resolve duplicate allocations. The former emphasizes distributed request ownership and constraints; the latter emphasizes construction of task bundles and conflict resolution.

Limited communication opportunities affect when coordination information arrives. Li et al. [38](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2020.02.018) include communication-contact prediction in synchronous and asynchronous consensus-based bundles, leading to different mission-value and messaging trade-offs as communication cost varies. Planning under delayed links [39](https://doi.org/10.1109/JSEE.2016.00066) treats latency explicitly; onboard coordination under uncertainty [40](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2021.09.003) combines contract-net, blackboard, and rolling-planning elements. A generic asynchronous task-allocation study [41](https://doi.org/10.24963/ijcai.2023/30) examines delays, losses, and dynamic events. The original satellite or generic network assumptions must be retained when these results are compared: an effective local plan still requires sufficiently timely and meaningful coordination messages.

### Integrated observation, processing, downlink, and delivery

Delivery depends on transmission after acquisition, so imaging and downlink opportunities are not independent. He et al. [42](https://doi.org/10.1109/TMC.2021.3076088) formulate observation and transmission windows as integer linear programming and combine semidefinite relaxation with genetic search. Shang et al. [43](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2025.02.008) add charging activities and use multiple-knapsack allocation, improved ant-colony search, and local improvement. Another integrated agile-observation model [44](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cor.2025.107212) schedules imaging and downlink jointly. An MILP formulation for heterogeneous tip-and-cue constellations [45](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2026.04.059) includes precedence between an initial detection and a subsequent focused observation. These models extend different resource or mission dependencies into current decisions; their rewards may correspond to acquisition, transmission, or completion of a linked sequence.

Data delivery additionally competes for shared ground facilities. Single-satellite/multi-station optimization [46](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cor.2014.11.004) converts potential contacts into schedules; scheduling of the Air Force Satellite Control Network [47](https://doi.org/10.1023/B:JOSH.0000013053.32600.3c) emphasizes competing requests; a downlink case study [48](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.omega.2015.01.001) incorporates detailed operating requirements. Deep Space Network MILP scheduling [49](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2021.3064928) formalizes antenna allocation, while Johnston et al. [50](https://doi.org/10.1609/aimag.v35i4.2552) describe automated expansion of user requests into tracking sessions with search, conflict repair, and operational negotiation. Although the spacecraft missions differ, these studies show that orbital visibility creates a possible contact; availability of an uncontested station reservation determines whether data can actually be transferred.

Public service interfaces describe completion and commitment states more explicitly. The Planet Tasking API [51](https://docs.planet.com/develop/apis/tasking/) differentiates acquisition, accepted quality, and delivered products, with further attempts permitted for certain flexible orders before expiry. AWS Ground Station [52](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/ground-station/latest/ug/contacts.html) stores the contact's mission-configuration snapshot; modifications of the reservation time or mission profile ordinarily require cancellation and a new reservation, whereas the versioning interface separately permits certain ephemeris or target updates within an existing contact. These are distinct forms of revision: quality-driven reacquisition and changes to already committed communication resources.

Onboard processing shifts the service chain toward generating useful information rather than merely recording images. The publicly available author version of Soret et al. [53](https://doi.org/10.1002/sat.70082), [author manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.05937v1), first selects acquisitions subject to time, attitude, and energy constraints, using image quality to determine observation value. It then uses acquired processing loads and downlink times as inputs to optimize allocation of computation between edge satellites and ground stations and associated operating frequencies, accounting for computation and communication energy. The model assumes a scheduled pipeline for distribution, processing, and collection and stable inter-satellite links. Results depend on processor characteristics, workload, and time slot. Acquisition schedules are inputs to downstream processing, and the study does not verify a fully joint, bidirectional adjustment of acquisition decisions in response to later computation. The corresponding published Wiley full text has not been compared with the author version at the same level of detail.

Where to execute computation is another resource-allocation problem. Multiagent offloading work [54](https://doi.org/10.1109/JIOT.2024.3482290), centralized-training/decentralized-execution edge computing [55](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.comcom.2024.05.008), and space–ground inference resource control [56](https://doi.org/10.1109/TMC.2025.3570799) integrate placement with different allocation or learning mechanisms. Orbit-aware scheduling [57](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10586-025-05663-9) represents orbital visibility in execution opportunities, while Kodan [58](https://doi.org/10.1145/3582016.3582043) uses offline-specialized models and runtime selection to address computational and downlink bottlenecks. Offloading changes where work occurs, orbit awareness changes when resources can be reached, and specialization changes how much computation is required per datum. Their contribution to observation services depends on the resulting information product and follow-on mission decisions.

### Replanning under dynamic requests and uncertainty

New requests may alter the feasible assignments and ordering of existing activities. Qin et al. [59](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2025.12.008) separate task assignment and ordering: a conflict-aware dynamic multiple-knapsack model with Lagrangian relaxation solves allocation, and backtracking/recursive repair adjusts sequences on arrival of additional requests. Simulations cover up to 1,000 satellites and 50–6,000 tasks and evaluate rewards and convergence time. Unlike branch-and-price for a defined task horizon [33](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cor.2025.107259), this hierarchical approach explicitly organizes reallocation and sequence repair around new task arrivals.

Uncertainty in clouds and image quality affects expected mission return before execution. Proactive scheduling [60](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cor.2016.04.014) uses chance constraints and sample approximation, robust scheduling [61](https://doi.org/10.1109/TAES.2019.2947978) employs uncertainty budgets and column generation, and stochastic scheduling [62](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejor.2018.11.043) represents scenarios and multistage mixed-integer decisions. Probability distributions, bounded uncertainty sets, and scenario information require different assumptions and induce different conservatism. Such anticipatory planning differs from rescheduling after the actual quality result becomes known.

Online learning methods can produce initial solutions, executable policies, or coordinating behavior. Qi et al. [63](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eswa.2025.128502) turn a rolling horizon into successive static subproblems, generate initial schedules with deep reinforcement learning, and improve them through large-neighborhood destroy-and-repair search. Rolling-horizon multiagent reinforcement learning [64](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eswa.2025.128350) uses centralized training and decentralized execution. Contact prediction [38](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2020.02.018) and contract-net coordination [40](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2021.09.003) instead connect updates explicitly to agent communication. The final online schedule emerges from different combinations of generation, search refinement, policy actions, and information exchange.

Herrmann et al. [65](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A35736) train one satellite policy and reuse it across members of a Walker constellation, avoiding some costs of directly training a joint multiagent policy. Their simulations compare unrestricted communication, one-hop line-of-sight, multihop line-of-sight, and no communication. Agents share states of already imaged or downlinked targets. Performance depends on network connectivity, and simultaneous duplicate selections can occur even with unrestricted sharing because future intended actions have not been coordinated. Thus policy reuse simplifies training without necessarily resolving all intersatellite conflicts.

Herrmann and Schaub [66](https://doi.org/10.3389/frspt.2023.1263489) compare PPO, resource-masked PPO, A2C, DQN, Monte Carlo tree-search training, and genetic algorithms in a single-satellite Basilisk environment. Returns, stability, and training time change with task complexity. Search-based training can produce strong schedules for more difficult agile tasks but incurs additional simulator and search cost. Alongside policy reuse, the study illustrates why training, online action production, and cross-satellite coordination must be accounted for separately.

### Servicing visits, transfers, and replenishment

Clients move along orbits, so a servicing sequence changes transfer duration, propellant cost, and replenishment opportunities. Multi-orbit service routing [67](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cie.2022.108852) models visits and refueling through a time-dependent network. Robust GEO refueling schedules [68](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cja.2025.103774) represent uncertain service durations using uncertainty budgets and optimize allocation and visiting order. Multiple-servicer planning [69](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2017.05.037) combines targets and transfers; mixed GEO refueling and debris-removal planning [70](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2024.05.059) contrasts stochastic and robust uncertainty models. Sequence decisions therefore depend on orbital dynamics, available propellant, and service time.

For low-thrust services, transfer costs depend on departure orbit, mass, and time. Apa et al. [72](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40295-025-00487-6) precompute transfer grids across orbital states, mass, and times and interpolate costs for combinatorial path search, considering perturbations, eclipses, and mass changes while omitting phasing-maneuver costs. In a related low-Earth-orbit drift study [71](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40295-026-00580-4), thrust–drift–thrust transfers are coupled with visits and refueling; a genetic algorithm optimizes paths using interpolated grids that account for J2, drag, eclipses, and mass evolution. Phasing costs are evaluated separately after the principal optimization. Both methods avoid repeatedly solving expensive transfer problems in the outer search, but the applicability of their route costs remains limited by the physical and terminal conditions represented in the grids.

Observation and servicing share assignment/ordering techniques, but their feasible activities are generated from different combinations of visibility, attitude, downstream resources, orbital transfers, and propellant. Table 4 summarizes representative studies.
