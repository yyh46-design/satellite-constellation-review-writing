# Intelligent Management and Scheduling of Large Satellite Constellations: Application Scenarios, Technical Approaches, and Operational Practice

## Abstract

Sustained operation of large satellite constellations requires coordination among dynamic service demand, finite resources, and changing spacecraft conditions. This problem-driven narrative review classifies existing work by application scenario and management problem and examines capacity planning and maintenance, Earth-observation and on-orbit-servicing scheduling, communication-network resource management, safe execution and recovery, and integrated operations. Representative methods are compared in terms of resource constraints, information structures, solution mechanisms, outcomes, and validation scope. Studies show that plan reuse and geometric decomposition can reduce computation and messaging costs, while their respective test cases also exhibit redundant observations or gaps in request satisfaction. Acquisition, processing, and delivery are coupled through joint selection, sequential planning, and operational feedback, with different decision scopes. Communication-resource allocation is conditioned by workload and capacity bottlenecks; the trade-off between route persistence and updates depends on link establishment costs. Independent measurements have identified service fluctuations at reconfiguration boundaries even in connection intervals with a single candidate serving satellite. Models, software prototypes, independent measurements, and flight tests provide distinct forms of evidence about mechanisms, implementation, and operations. We synthesize the trade-offs among computation, information, and service quality and identify research needs concerning service recovery after degradation, the scope of end-to-end decisions, updates under delayed information, and validation under shared operational scenarios.

**Keywords:** large satellite constellations; intelligent management; mission scheduling; satellite communication networks; resource allocation; autonomous operations

# 1 Introduction

## 1.1 Expanding constellation services and the need for sustained operations

Large constellations provide observation and communication services through coordinated satellites in multiple orbital planes and associated ground facilities. Larger fleets and mission sets increase candidate activities, while resource competition, status updates, and failure recovery introduce substantial management interactions. The survey of automated operations for distributed spacecraft [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2020.08.009) analyzes changing operational practices, staffing and tools, and scalability. Constellation management accordingly extends beyond individual activity scheduling to the provision of sustained capability, allocation of missions, and updates of execution states.

Earth observation and communication networks have different service processes. Observation requests typically include targets, time windows, priorities, and quality criteria; scheduling addresses acquisition, processing, and delivery. Communication traffic arrives continuously; orbital motion and changing demand affect access, link availability, and load, so beams, spectrum, power, associations, and routes must be adjusted over time. Observation output is assessed using acquisitions, usable products, or on-time delivery, while communication output is measured through throughput, latency, outages, and fairness. Although some resource-allocation techniques are shared, completion events, competitive interactions, and performance metrics are application specific.

Spacecraft availability and execution states further constrain service. Deployment, spare capacity, and replenishment determine future capabilities; collision-avoidance maneuvers, component faults, and derating change present opportunities and capacity. On-orbit servicing and space logistics introduce additional maintenance and recovery actions [2](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A35982). Asset number and location, recovery time, and maintenance cost thus link operational task feasibility to the provision, preservation, and restoration of constellation capability.

## 1.2 Existing reviews and the analytical focus of this article

Prior reviews have organized mission scheduling, distributed coordination, and automated operations in considerable detail. Wang et al. [3](https://doi.org/10.1109/JSYST.2020.2997050) classify agile Earth-observation models, constraints, variants, and solution methods; Ferrari et al. [4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cor.2024.106875) distinguish imaging, communication, and integrated scheduling in Earth and outer-space observation. The communication activities in such observation studies mainly concern downlink and ground-facility use, rather than continuous broadband service in large communication networks. A review of agent-based approaches [5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2025.10.018) covers distributed spacecraft through agents, architectures, coordination, and mission management, while surveys of automation paradigms and tools [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2020.08.009), [6](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2020.08.018) examine operational responsibility and implementation.

Communication-specific reviews address sustained network service and resource management. The accessible abstracts of two Chinese-language surveys [7](https://doi.org/10.7527/S1000-6893.2026.32935), [8](https://doi.org/10.3873/j.issn.1000-1328.2026.02.002) list demand prediction, topology/mobility management, access, routing, resource modeling, control architectures, and dynamic optimization. A review of integrated satellite operations and network management [9](https://doi.org/10.3390/technologies13080312) explicitly treats SatOps–NetOps coupling. The contribution is not a renaming of existing categories. We compare research using common but explicit descriptors—service objective, decision variables, information conditions, and completion events. For algorithms addressing the same formulation and methods addressing different problems, we distinguish effects of mechanism, baseline, and workload. Across operational functions, we identify the actual scope of joint decisions, sequential inputs, and feedback repair. These comparisons provide conditional technical judgments rather than a universal ranking across heterogeneous algorithms.

Literature is therefore cross-classified by application scenario and management problem. Long-term planning and safety recovery support both applications, while observation tasks and communication-network resources are distinct scheduling objects. Integrated studies address dependencies among capability, resources, and execution. Research papers establish models and experimental evidence; public operational materials and flight demonstrations describe service states, decision authority, and executed actions.

## 1.3 Review scope and method

This article is a problem-driven narrative technical review based on earlier topic searches, reference tracing, and surveys, with targeted additional work on communication networks. It emphasizes technical developments, formulations, and conditions of applicability; no exhaustive reproducible database-screening protocol was performed. Accordingly, we make no claims about population-wide method frequencies or pooled effects across the literature.

Coverage includes capability provision, mission and network-resource scheduling, safe execution, anomaly recovery, and their operating dependencies. Earth observation and communications are the principal services; on-orbit servicing, logistics, and collision-risk management are included according to management function. Modulation, device design, and generic signal processing appear only when their constraints matter for constellation-scale decisions. Single-satellite, GEO, deep-space, and generic multiagent work is used as methodological context with domain and information restrictions retained.

“Large” is interpreted through scaling burdens in satellite/request counts, candidate activities, resource coupling, messages, and time horizon, rather than a single threshold. Intelligent management includes optimization, knowledge reasoning, distributed coordination, and learning-based prediction or policy generation; actual autonomy depends on decision and execution authority. Operational practice refers to publicly described workflows, service APIs, software, and flight demonstrations.

For representative studies, we extract problem objects, variables, constraints, available information, solution and updating mechanisms, outcomes, and verification settings. Comparisons preserve original workloads and assumptions. Modeling studies, simulations, software/hardware tests, flight demonstrations, and operating records offer different forms of evidence. Review-author classifications and syntheses must not be mistaken for experiments performed by the cited studies.
