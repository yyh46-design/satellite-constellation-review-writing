## 3.4 Safe Execution, Anomaly Recovery, and Service Resilience

Executing a mission schedule requires compliance with continuous orbital dynamics, spacecraft health, and external safety constraints. Conjunction screening identifies candidate risk events, trajectory optimization and control produce actions subject to safety requirements, and anomaly response or plan repair updates the resources available for later service. Each activity addresses a distinct part of sustained operations under disturbances.

### Close-approach detection and risk information

Large-scale conjunction assessment incurs costs both in filtering candidate object pairs and in accurately finding local minima of separation. Chebyshev proxy polynomials [82](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2020.01.020) approximate motion over subintervals and locate separation extrema to estimate closest approach; achieved accuracy depends on polynomial order, interval length, and error control. Saingyen et al. [84](https://doi.org/10.3390/aerospace10060543) evaluate Smart Sieve, CSieve, and a combined CAOS-D candidate filter with CUDA computation for one-satellite-versus-catalog and catalog-pair searches. GPU computation reduces costs in the tested datasets, whereas Smart Sieve misses some events under those conditions; the other filters maintain the reported corresponding screening results. A proxy polynomial reduces the cost of resolving a candidate event, while a sieve changes how many pairs reach detailed examination. Their efficiency should therefore be reported together with event-retention and miss results.

The acquisition of risk information is itself a resource-allocation task. Risk-aware sensor scheduling [83](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2021.04.042) uses information value and collision risk to prioritize object tracking, connecting sensing to conjunction assessment. Later stages have distinct objectives: a machine-learning collision-avoidance competition [87](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42064-021-0101-5) predicts eventual risk from conjunction data messages, the CAMmary survey [86](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2025.07.027) compares maneuver-design methods, and a constellation simulation framework [85](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12567-022-00471-y) explores operator decisions about who should maneuver and when. These are respectively information, action-design, and coordination components of collision-risk management.

The CCSDS Conjunction Data Message standard [88](https://ccsds.org/publications/bluebooks/entry/3064/) specifies data exchanged among organizations. Starlink's public collision-avoidance concept of operations [89](https://space-safety.starlink.com/docs/space-safety-articles/starlink_cola_conops/) describes repeated ephemeris generation, screening, onboard risk reassessment, and insertion, modification, or cancellation of maneuvers according to maneuverability and event status. Risk updates can thus alter control actions and, through them, existing mission plans. The publication of such an operating process is not an independent proof of the internal numerical algorithm's miss probability.

### Safety-constrained trajectories and feedback control

Trajectory planners represent separation, line-of-sight, and terminal requirements as dynamical constraints. Passive-safe rendezvous trajectories [90](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.29590) investigate intrinsic safety under defined conditions; conic optimization [91](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.58436) provides an optimization approach to autonomous proximity trajectories; model predictive control (MPC) using sequential convex programming [92](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.G000218) updates multiple-spacecraft trajectories while exchanging neighborhood information. Safety geometry, optimization structure, and feedback are different means of handling continuous motion after discrete mission scheduling.

Chance-constrained control expresses risk bounds under modeled uncertainty. Gavilan et al. [93](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.conengprac.2011.09.006) use Hill–Clohessy–Wiltshire relative dynamics and Gaussian additive disturbances, converting chance constraints in an MPC rendezvous problem to algebraic conditions and comparing nominal and chance-constrained controllers in Monte Carlo simulation. A subsequent study of noncooperative visual rendezvous [94](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ast.2025.111237) includes coupled attitude and translational motion, image-based feedback, recursive disturbance estimation, and probabilistic constraint tightening to address tumbling, model error, and measurement noise. Both model uncertainty explicitly, but their protected states and assumed dynamics differ; guarantees cannot be carried across those systems without the corresponding assumptions.

The time domain over which a safety constraint is enforced matters as well. Continuous-time successive convexification [95](https://doi.org/10.2514/6.2026-2446) addresses passively safe rendezvous on a near-rectilinear halo orbit. The physical environment and safety conditions differ from typical LEO constellations, so this is a methodological reference rather than direct verification of LEO safety.

Comparison under the same inspection task can reveal differences between learning and optimization. Stephenson and Schaub [96](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40295-026-00631-w) study shielded, closed-loop reinforcement learning and open-loop trajectories produced using probabilistic roadmaps and mixed-integer quadratically constrained planning. The shield restricts collision-prone actions using relative dynamics, whereas the roadmap searches candidate inspection paths satisfying geometric conditions. In high-fidelity simulations, the roadmap approach offers favorable fuel–time trade-offs at higher offline computational cost, while the learning strategy produces feedback actions with lower online burden. The results depend on task geometry, safety restrictions, and open- versus closed-loop execution.

The ADRAS-J flight event illustrates the distinction between nominal proximity operations and abort capability [97](https://www.astroscale.com/en/news/astroscales-adras-j-achieves-historic-15-meter-approach-to-space-debris). On 30 November 2024, the vehicle approached a noncooperative rocket stage to approximately 15 m before an anomalous relative-attitude condition triggered an autonomous abort and safe retreat. The reported observation objectives and successful safety response must be distinguished from additional approach or capture objectives that were not completed. The event shows that execution-state changes can alter a mission without reducing its outcome to a binary success label.

### Anomaly recognition, fault isolation, and plan repair

Telemetry anomaly detection compares measured and expected states. Hundman et al. [98](https://doi.org/10.1145/3219819.3219845) apply long short-term memory (LSTM) prediction errors and dynamic thresholds to SMAP/MSL anomaly records. Bayesian LSTM work [99](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2020.12.012) considers imbalanced telemetry and prediction uncertainty, and validation-gated continual learning [100](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2026.07.065) studies model updates over time. A subsequent reevaluation [101](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12567-023-00529-5) shows that reported anomaly-detection rankings depend on metrics, postprocessing, and simple baselines. Detection supplies evidence of abnormal behavior but does not itself diagnose a fault or enact recovery.

Rule-based systems connect state conditions to possible actions. Wanninger [102](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12567-025-00651-6) uses CLIPS to organize state facts, fault classification, and responses, with an obstacle-navigation demonstration on a terrestrial RaspTank rover. Generation and rule processing for approximately 30 state parameters take milliseconds on a Raspberry Pi prototype; the performance of a complete flight FDIR configuration on representative space hardware remains to be evaluated. Relative to prediction-error detection, this provides explicit fault-to-action mappings, with limitations from rule coverage, state accuracy, and execution hardware.

A failure can also invalidate previously scheduled activities and resource assignments. The Schedule Manager of Gillette and George [103](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.I011208) applies time windows, priorities, conflict types, and resource constraints and loads predefined contingency plans after selected failures. Integration with core Flight System and unit, functional, code, and development-board tests assess the software. A reactive plan repair strategy for deep-space missions (RPRS) [104](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2020.05.011) instead diagnoses remaining resources and repairs or reschedules affected activities. The first method switches to prepared responses, whereas the second adjusts plans using the remaining operational state; both must be interpreted within their tested missions and platforms.

Anomaly detection, rule response, and plan repair produce respectively warnings, recovery actions, and revised schedules. A restored component changes availability, while restoration of service must be measured by resumed mission outcomes. Table 6 contrasts immediate outputs and validation conditions.
