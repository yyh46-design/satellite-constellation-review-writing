# 5 Conclusions

The management and scheduling of large satellite constellations involve capability provision, service-resource allocation, and execution or recovery. By cross-classifying the literature according to application and management problem, this review has examined Earth-observation scheduling and communication-network control and placed spare maintenance, on-orbit servicing, and safety recovery among the supporting functions shared by these services. An observation mission progresses through acquisition, processing, and delivery, whereas a communication network sustains traffic through demand-aware access, beam allocation, and route management. Both contend with varying resources and coordination, but their workload and completion criteria have different service meanings.

Research has developed multiple mechanisms for addressing computational and informational costs. Incremental planning and neighborhood decomposition can reduce search or message overhead, but specific experiments retain duplicate observations or gaps in request satisfaction. Sharing only completed-state information can leave simultaneous competition unresolved. Connections across observation tasks include per-request joint selection of acquisition and delivery paths, passing an existing acquisition plan to a downstream computation model, and replanning after operational feedback. Joint decisions, staged planning, and feedback have different decision variables and information requirements; their value depends on whether downstream constraints materially affect present feasibility and outcomes.

Communication-management studies show load-dependent trade-offs in allocation and route updates. Beam and power strategies respond differently to traffic and capacity bottlenecks, and single-satellite allocation, multisatellite association, and global spectrum management coordinate resources at distinct scopes. Retaining paths versus reconfiguring them involves link-setup delay and route lifetime: instant shortest paths need not minimize average delay after update costs are included. Independent Starlink measurements also show fluctuations near network reconfiguration boundaries when only one serving satellite is available, demonstrating that service state may change without a handover.

Mathematical simulations, software prototypes, published business interfaces, independent measurements, and flight demonstrations furnish different evidence. Models explain decision mechanisms; prototypes test implementations; interfaces specify commitment and completion states; measurements and flight trials show behavior in particular circumstances. Research connecting maintenance to service losses, deciding when end-to-end joint optimization is worthwhile, and updating plans under delayed information or resource commitments can be evaluated using shared traffic, mission, and disturbance scenarios. This would clarify when separately effective methods remain useful and operationally consistent within sustained constellation services.
