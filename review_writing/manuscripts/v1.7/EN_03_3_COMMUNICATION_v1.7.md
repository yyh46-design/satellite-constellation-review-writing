## 3.3 Communication-Constellation Network Management and Resource Scheduling

LEO communication networks combine time-varying demand with changing topology. Demand varies across regions and times, while orbital motion modifies coverage, access associations, and end-to-end paths. Under limited beams, spectrum, power, gateways, and intersatellite links, resource management aims to match traffic with usable service capacity. Related research addresses demand prediction, beam and capacity allocation, handover, routing, and interdomain control. These subproblems respectively provide forecasts, resolve local resource competition, carry service traffic, and organize wider coordination.

### Demand prediction and beam, power, and spectrum allocation

Spatially uneven demand makes fixed illumination and uniform allocation inefficient under changing loads. Beam hopping time-shares satellite beams among cells and involves both discrete illumination decisions and continuous power allocation. Han et al. [73](https://doi.org/10.3390/aerospace11030191) combine empirical mode decomposition, ARIMA, and an optimized neural network to forecast self-similar traffic generated from synthetic ON/OFF processes. The output is a workload estimate usable by a later allocator; its prediction accuracy does not itself demonstrate improved end-to-end routing or throughput. Beam and power optimization subsequently distributes constrained capacity, typically evaluating traffic–capacity mismatch, throughput, and delay rather than forecast error.

Joint beam scheduling and power optimization [74](https://doi.org/10.23919/JCC.ja.2022-0864) uses a decomposed approach: a potential game selects illuminated cells under an average-power assumption, and a penalty interior-point procedure optimizes continuous power after beam choices are fixed. The [public preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01292v1) considers one satellite with eight beams and 61 possible beam positions and minimizes squared supply–demand mismatch. Its G-BH and G-BHPO comparisons use the same greedy beam-selection rule but uniform versus optimized power. The latter has lower mismatch in the examined cases, suggesting that power allocation influences service distribution even when the greedy beam-selection rule is shared. It does not establish a globally optimal joint illumination/power solution, and the journal full text has not been directly compared with this author version.

Overlapping multisatellite coverage introduces cell association and coordinated illumination. Lin et al. [75](https://doi.org/10.1109/LCOMM.2022.3213912) divide NGSO beam hopping into satellite–cell assignment and joint beam-illumination design. The assignment stage accounts for satellite-load balance and interference toward GSO earth stations, while illumination is iteratively optimized using a co-channel interference criterion. This two-stage characterization follows the formal IEEE abstract; exact integer variables, all interference terms, and experimental baselines have not yet been verified in the full article. Zhao et al. [76](https://doi.org/10.1109/TWC.2025.3545745) integrate a digital twin and multiagent learning, using predicted demand and queue states to assign beams and satellite-level power. The [specified author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08896v1) models 12 satellites, four beams per satellite, and 168 cells, with constraints on service association and power budgets. Multisatellite models must consider overlapping coverage and coordinated assignments, but interference structures differ between studies; one paper's association decisions cannot be substituted for the complete physical interference constraints of another.

Performance gains depend on offered load. In the author-version experiments of Zhao et al., demand-aware beam selection with fixed equal power (FPA) and digital-twin-based selection with flexible discrete power (DPA) achieve similar throughput under 8–20 Gbit/s of aggregate offered demand. Above 20 Gbit/s, DPA obtains higher throughput in the tested system. The study attributes the change to the growing value of power flexibility under high load. Because the two methods also differ in decision organization, this is a comparison of complete strategies, not a clean ablation of the power module alone. Alongside single-satellite power optimization, these results indicate that changing beam, association, or power decisions is most consequential when the corresponding resource is a capacity bottleneck.

These studies decompose and coordinate different variables. [74](https://doi.org/10.23919/JCC.ja.2022-0864) and [76](https://doi.org/10.1109/TWC.2025.3545745) consider discrete beams and continuous or discretized power, while [75](https://doi.org/10.1109/LCOMM.2022.3213912) stages cell association and illumination. They address single-satellite demand matching, multisatellite allocation, and external coexistence conditions, respectively. Learning transfers some cost to training and requires available global or local state. Applicability therefore depends on coordination scope, information availability, and online computation.

### Earth–space access, handover, and service continuity

LEO motion forces active terminals to change serving satellites, beams, or gateways. Highest-elevation or strongest-signal selection evaluates immediate link quality, whereas sustained service is also affected by path latency, network load, and connection-holding time. Keeping a connection longer may reduce handover frequency but sacrifice short-term path quality. Handover thus decides both whom to select and how long a service association should persist.

Yin et al. [77](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cja.2024.09.039) analytically estimate end-to-end propagation delay and include it in a multiobjective handover formulation. The single-layer decision uses Dueling Double DQN; interlayer decisions additionally use jitter prediction and handover costs. The freely accessible formal publication abstract reports latency and jitter improvements by the combined strategy relative to selected baselines, but the final full text has not been available for section-by-section verification of state, action, reward, scale, or module ablation. The result is accordingly interpreted as a combined path-aware prediction-and-learning design, without assigning its observed benefit to any one component.

Mohan et al. [78](https://doi.org/10.1145/3589334.3645328) measured Starlink performance across countries and in controlled terminal experiments in the United Kingdom and Germany, observing latency and throughput changes around 15-second reconfiguration boundaries. Under a restricted UK terminal field of view, fluctuations remained during windows with only one candidate serving satellite. A satellite handover is therefore not necessary to explain every observed change. The authors suggest periodic scheduling or network reconfiguration, but they did not directly observe the operator's internal resource-allocation mechanisms. Service continuity includes network state changes while an access association is retained, not merely the handover event itself.

### Dynamic topology, intersatellite routing, and congestion recovery

Routing-update cost depends on network architecture and link formation. Networks without direct intersatellite links rely on terrestrial relays, while laser ISL networks are constrained by pointing, acquisition, and establishment time. Both have time-varying paths, but differ in available links, management roles, and update costs.

Zhang and Yeung [79](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.comcom.2022.02.015) propose a core–edge routing architecture for an LEO network primarily connected through ground stations rather than direct ISLs. Publisher-visible method excerpts distinguish delay-bounded routing (DBR), which maintains flow-based forwarding state to preserve latency bounds but requires larger tables, from delay-aware routing (DAR), which aggregates forwarding by destination and reduces updates without maintaining the same strict per-flow bound. These mechanisms can be checked from accessible publisher descriptions, but their full numerical results and signaling costs remain unverified. Zhang et al. [80](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcan.2022.09.022) use segment routing for traffic engineering and fast rerouting in a ground-relayed network. DBTS and DBTS+ differ in load-splitting preferences, and LFA/LFA+ provide alternative degrees of failure backup. The formal abstract supports the method categories and case-specific results, not a claim of optimizing physical link capacity.

Bhattacharjee et al. [121](https://doi.org/10.1109/TAES.2024.3415571) include laser-link establishment delay in path cost and compare instantaneous shortest paths, preserving installed paths, and stability-/activity-aware alternatives. Their [public author version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01953v1) uses 1,584 satellites and 600 one-second network snapshots. When link establishment is costly, retaining existing routes or considering active links can avoid part of the repeated setup burden. With very low setup cost, some persistence strategies have slightly higher average delay than instant shortest-path routing in the cited scenarios. More extensive time-dependent evaluation also increases computation. The trade-off thus involves path lifetime, establishment time, and frequency of reconfiguration.

That study moves beyond instantaneous route length to path cost including the act of updating links. It assumes a controller has already computed and distributed the relevant routes, uniform establishment delay, and no queuing or initial configuration delay. Its results therefore isolate route persistence and link establishment under those assumptions. Ground-relayed traffic engineering and failure recovery use different network and capacity models. Route stability, congestion, and resilience must be judged in relation to link formation, resource contention, and activation of control information.

### Interdomain resource allocation and control responsibility

A centralized, frequently updated view of all resources can impose computation and message costs, whereas local scheduling must still coordinate cross-domain spectrum and interference. Gou et al. [81](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25030902) distinguish the network control center, space base stations, and user terminals as management levels. Global service-region and spectrum settings constrain local beams, slots, and channels, while demand and price exchanges connect configuration to localized service. This hierarchy supports wide-area coordination and more responsive local decisions.

Its published component comparisons cover spectrum management, user scheduling, and beam allocation under different loads and evaluation settings. The performance of each component demonstrates its role in the corresponding conditions; the entire deployed system's response additionally depends on information exchanges and configuration activation. Centralized route control similarly relies on timely information: the dynamic laser-link model [121](https://doi.org/10.1109/TAES.2024.3415571) presumes routes are computed and distributed in advance. A hierarchy therefore defines both responsibilities and the information and operating authority available to local agents.

Communication-constellation research reveals three recurring interactions: coupling of scarce resources, maintaining service continuity, and accounting for route updates. Joint beam/power allocation depends on the bottleneck and coordination scope; handovers and network changes during a held connection affect service differently; and route persistence must be evaluated together with setup cost. These results motivate cross-domain coordination of beams, access, routes, and control.
