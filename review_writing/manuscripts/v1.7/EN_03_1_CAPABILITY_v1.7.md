# 3 Technical Approaches to Large-Constellation Management and Scheduling

Research on constellation management spans capability provision, service-resource allocation, safe execution, and recovery. These functions interact through asset availability, activity dependencies, and operational feedback. This section organizes representative work by management function and application, comparing decision variables, solution mechanisms, reported results, and applicable conditions.

## 3.1 Long-Term Capability Configuration and Sustained Maintenance

Long-term configuration affects day-to-day scheduling through asset locations, readiness times, and failure-recovery options. Constellation design determines where future capacity is available, spare and replenishment policies provide replacements after failures, and servicing infrastructure determines which clients can be reached. The space-logistics review [2](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A35982) combines network flows, inventory, and operations, highlighting dependencies in time and resources among these decisions.

### Constellation selection, deployment, and reconfiguration

Coverage benefit and orbital transfer cost are key competing objectives in reconfiguration. Appel et al. [11](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2014.02.016) optimize maneuvers for a specified target constellation. Lee and Ho [12](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A35685) jointly choose regional coverage configurations and satellite assignments, allowing the target pattern to vary with the transfer-cost trade-off. Multistage reconfiguration [13](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A35990) adds configurations and transfers across successive stages. These formulations differ in whether the final pattern is fixed, optimized, or selected at multiple stages; the deterministic multistage model assumes that future demand over its stages is specified in advance.

Deployment requirements further restrict available patterns. Complex regional pattern design [14](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A34657) uses cyclic convolutions of access and constellation patterns in a binary programming model, while optimization under uncertain areas of interest [15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2018.03.054) compares designs across demand scenarios. Gwon and Ahn [16](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42405-026-01298-1) include parking duration, J2-induced right-ascension-of-ascending-node (RAAN) drift, launch capacity, and deployment deadlines in a two-stage design. Their RAAN feasibility condition is a design-level screen rather than a fully resolved launch trajectory or proof of actual spacecraft arrival. Coverage performance and deployability therefore answer different questions, while transfer and deployment schedules influence when capacity becomes usable.

### Communication platforms, capacity, and gateway configuration

Geometric coverage and network-service capacity are distinct design dimensions. Even under the same coverage geometry, spacecraft power, antennas and beams, spatial traffic patterns, and transport routes can create different bottlenecks. Joint platform–constellation design [17](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.comnet.2024.110942) incorporates beam hopping, antenna and platform parameters, orbits, and fleet size, connecting short-term resource configuration to long-term architecture. Its comparisons of low- and very-low-Earth-orbit solutions hold for the demand, coverage, hardware, and cost models studied.

Long-term design establishes the capacity envelope, while beam scheduling and routing allocate it across places and times. An increase in geometric coverage need not eliminate overload in high-demand regions. Payload design, gateways, and potential routes all contribute to usable network throughput. Configuration and operational resource allocation are consequently interdependent communication-management decisions.

### Spare inventories, replenishment, and recovery of failed capabilities

Random failures make long-term maintenance an inventory and replenishment problem. Jakob et al. [18](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A34387) describe ground, parking-orbit, and in-plane spares as a multiechelon inventory network, jointly selecting inventory amounts and order triggers while including waiting times caused by relative orbital drift between planes. The analytic model is evaluated through 25 sampled parameter cases and repeated simulations spanning many years. In-plane spares are assumed to replace failed members immediately; this is a specific approximation of that replacement stage, not an omission of all replenishment lead times. Inventory fill rates therefore describe supply performance, while service restoration may additionally depend on repositioning and commissioning conditions.

Subsequent models refine supply choices and stochastic inventory dynamics. A dual-supply replenishment strategy [19](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A36281) considers transfer from parking orbit and direct injection into the operational plane. Han et al. [20](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A36599) use Markov chains for in-plane and parking-orbit inventory and a fixed-point iteration to obtain mutually consistent stationary states; an extension [21](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2026.06.020) addresses direct and indirect replenishment channels. These developments refine supply lead times and long-term costs from different perspectives. Stationary indices summarize average supply behavior, however, whereas the restoration of service following a particular fault unfolds over a time-dependent sequence of actions.

Iridium's public record of deploying five additional spare satellites [22](https://investor.iridium.com/2023-05-20-Iridium-Adds-to-Constellation-Resilience-with-Launch-of-Spare-Satellites) confirms that spare capacity is a practical constellation asset. The number of spares quantifies redundancy, while orbital location and transfer conditions determine when it can be used. Thus equal inventory investment need not yield equal interruption time, which is an important connection between inventory policy and operational scheduling.

On-orbit servicing extends the possible responses to failed assets. Kim et al. [23](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A36684) jointly consider new-satellite deployment and service-based restoration, representing operator service levels and maintenance costs alongside provider prices and capacities in a bi-objective framework. In their assumed failure, price, and service scenarios, selected policies reduce annual maintenance cost by up to about 14.5% relative to a no-servicing baseline. This case-dependent result is influenced by the fraction of failures that can be repaired and by pricing; it cannot be extrapolated to all LEO constellations without the corresponding cost and reachability assumptions.

### Servicing infrastructure and uncertain demand

Orbital depot positions and customer assignments determine what maintenance services are reachable. Shimane et al. [24](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A35691) jointly choose depots, customer allocations, and propulsion demand. Choi and Ho [25](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A36386) additionally optimize depot orbits within a continuous domain and express depot location and client order as a mixed-integer nonlinear problem solved by alternating discrete and continuous steps. Their case studies include servicing of the GPS constellation. Discrete candidate locations preserve a network location structure; continuous orbit locations add design freedom but increase coupling between position and transfer cost.

Demand uncertainty and propulsion also affect infrastructure decisions. A servicing-vehicle deployment method [26](https://doi.org/10.1155/2021/6644339) evaluates advance positioning against potential client reachability. A framework for uncertain service demand [27](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A34978) updates operations and logistics using a rolling horizon. A high-/low-thrust trade-off framework [28](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.A35094) embeds user-defined trajectory costs in mixed-integer linear programming and explores effects on short-term schedules and long-term depot decisions. Lifecycle assessment [29](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2013.02.005) distinguishes aleatory and epistemic uncertainties. These models refine different aspects—locations, update schedules, transfer times, or demand knowledge—and show why a useful representation of service capability depends on asset states and planning horizon.

Overall, long-term configuration studies investigate how investments become usable services. Deployment, replenishment waits, and servicing interventions determine the conditions under which future activities are possible. Constellation, inventory, and depot models capture different parts of this process, with different ranges of physical and operational detail.
